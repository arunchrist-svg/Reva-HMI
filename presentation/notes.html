Session 1: Human–Computer Interaction foundations

Arun Murugesan · Head of Design, Moneyview. Shared after studio. Use these definitions in examinations.

1. Human–Computer Interaction (HCI)

DefinitionHuman–Computer Interaction is the study of how people interact with computational systems, and of how those systems should be designed so that interaction is effective, efficient, and satisfying.

HCI is not limited to desktop computers. It includes kiosks, mobile devices, building interfaces, wearables, and physical objects that mediate a digital or transactional task (for example a wallet used to complete a payment).

HCI draws on computer science, psychology, ergonomics, and design. In architecture, the same questions apply when a spatial programme includes screens, access control, wayfinding, or service delivery.

2. User experience (UX)

Definition (ISO 9241-210)User experience is a person’s perceptions and responses that result from the use and/or anticipated use of a product, system, or service.

Perceptions and responses include emotion, belief, preference, physical and psychological responses, behaviour, and accomplishment. They occur before use (expectation), during use, and after use (memory, trust, willingness to return).

Don Norman popularised the term to cover more than usability of a screen: the entire encounter with a product or service, including industrial design, graphics, interface, interaction, and manual.

What UX is not

  • UX is not visual styling, colour, or “making it look modern.”
  • UX is not the same as UI (see §3).
  • UX cannot be claimed from the designer’s intention; it is observed in use.

How UX is judged

A design succeeds when a representative user can complete the intended task with acceptable effort, without unexplained help. If the designer must speak for the product to work, the experience has failed.

3. User interface (UI)

DefinitionA user interface is the point of contact through which a person operates a product or system: the controls, displays, materials, layout, and feedback that make action possible.

UI may be graphical (screens, buttons, type), physical (folds, slots, handles, kiosks), or mixed. UI is a subset of the larger experience. A well-finished interface can still produce a poor experience if the task structure is wrong.

Examination line: UI is the means of interaction. UX is the consequence of interaction over time.

5. Usability

Definition (ISO 9241-11)Usability is the extent to which a system, product, or service can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction in a specified context of use.

  • Effectiveness — accuracy and completeness of goal achievement.
  • Efficiency — resources expended (time, steps, errors) relative to completeness.
  • Satisfaction — freedom from discomfort and positive attitudes toward use.

Usability is a major component of UX, not the whole of UX. UX also includes anticipation, brand trust, and after-use memory.

6. Human-centered design (HCD)

Definition (Don Norman)Human-centered design is an approach that puts people and their needs at the centre of the process: (1) focus on people, (2) solve the right problem, (3) think of the work as a system, not an isolated object.

HCD refuses to treat a symptom (an ugly fold, an ugly screen) when the root problem is elsewhere (anxiety in a queue, unclear payment sequence). Local optimisation of one part can worsen the whole system.

7. Core terms

AffordanceA perceived possibility for action. The object should communicate how it can be used (a slot that reads as “card goes here”; a fold that reads as “open here”). Coined for HCI by Norman, after Gibson.

SignifierA perceivable signal (label, mark, shape) that points to an affordance. Affordances exist in the object; signifiers make them discoverable.

FeedbackInformation returned to the user after an action, confirming what the system did (a note seated in a slot; a screen stating “paid”).

Mental modelThe user’s internal explanation of how something works. Design should match that model, or teach a better one without a spoken tour.

Primary taskThe single goal against which the design is tested. Example: “Someone can pay at the canteen without emptying the purse.” Not “redesign money.”

Error / failure stateThe designed behaviour when the happy path breaks: wrong denomination, empty slot, jammed fold. Errors are part of the interface, not an afterthought.

PrototypeA testable representation of a design, at any fidelity. Paper is a valid prototype if a person can attempt the task with it.

Usability test (stranger test)An empirical method: a person who did not design the artefact attempts specified tasks while thinking aloud. The facilitator does not instruct. Observation is logged (success, failure, location of hesitation).

8. Process: Evidence-to-Experience

Design decisions must be traceable to observation, not preference.

  1. Observe — interview or watch a real person performing a real task. Do not ask “Would you like this?”
  2. State the task — “A [user] can [goal] without [barrier].”
  3. Prototype — make only what is needed to attempt the task (here: a physical paper wallet or money purse).
  4. Test — silent trial; log done / stuck / where.
  5. Iterate — change the artefact from the log; test again.

A definition that has not been tested in use remains a hypothesis.

9. User research (Session 1)

DefinitionUser research is evidence gathering about real behaviour, context, and needs — before you design for someone else.

In the studio, version 03 of the paper wallet is built for one person you interviewed, not for yourself. Research is not asking “Do you like my wallet?”

How to interview (in class)

  • Choose a classmate or someone in the room.
  • Ask about past behaviour: paying, carrying cash, coins, cards, embarrassment in a queue.
  • Write short quotes you can point to when you fold version 03.

Listen for

  • The pay task from start to finish
  • Friction, delay, or embarrassment in public
  • What must stay separate (notes, change, cards)
  • How they know the right payment left the purse

10. Session 1 studio — three wallet versions

Students design a physical paper wallet or money purse three times from issued paper (tape, scissors):

  1. Version 01 — before any UX definition is taught (often for themselves).
  2. Version 02 — rebuilt after user experience is defined (ISO 9241-210): task, journey, error — not decoration.
  3. Version 03 — after user research: interview someone in the room, then rebuild the wallet for that person from what they said.

All three versions stay visible. Learning is the difference between them and the final one-sentence definition of UX.

11. Suggested examination answers

Define UX. User experience (ISO 9241-210) is a person’s perceptions and responses resulting from the use or anticipated use of a product, system, or service, including emotion, behaviour, and accomplishment before, during, and after use.

Distinguish UX and UI. UI is the interface — the visible and tangible means of control. UX is the experienced outcome of using that interface in context. UI is necessary but not sufficient for good UX.

What is usability? The extent to which specified users can achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction in a specified context (ISO 9241-11).

What is an affordance? A perceived possibility for action offered by an object; in design, the object should make appropriate actions discoverable, often with signifiers.
