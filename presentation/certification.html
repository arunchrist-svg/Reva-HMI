Executive Summary

This proposal describes a 36-hour, 3-credit Certificate Program in UI/UX Design open to students and professionals from any academic background. No prior design experience is required. Participants who complete the program earn the designation Certified UI/UX Designer, issued by REVA University.

The program addresses where UX is heading — not just where it has been. Participants engage with AI-native design workflows, human-machine interaction for automated environments, invisible and Zero-UI paradigms, and frameworks for ethical, biophilic, and bias-aware design. Every session is grounded in a hands-on project; every design decision must be supported by observation, data, or structured critique.

The program is task-based and evidence-backed. Every session produces a deliverable. Participants leave with a tested prototype, a documented research and iteration process, an industry-standard portfolio case study, and direct exposure to practitioners working at the frontier of design through structured Expert Sessions embedded in the curriculum.

I, Arun Murugesan, bring 18+ years of product design practice across SAP, Cisco, Rupeek, and MoneyView, an MS in Computer Software Engineering from BITS Pilani, and current visiting faculty engagement at REVA University (M25DA304D, HCI, M.Arch III Semester). This program extends that practice into an open-access certification that prepares participants for where the industry is moving.

02

Program Objectives

By the end of this program, participants will be able to:

  1. Plan and conduct user research — structured interviews, contextual observation, card sorting — and synthesize raw data into actionable design insights.
  2. Build information architectures and task flows that reflect how real users think, not how product teams think.
  3. Apply foundational UX principles — Norman's affordances, Nielsen's heuristics, WCAG 2.1 AA — to audit and improve any digital interface.
  4. Design in Figma from wireframe to high-fidelity prototype, using components and design systems.
  5. Run usability tests with real users and document measurable outcomes: task success rate, time-on-task, error rate.
  6. Iterate from evidence — make design decisions that follow from test data, not from personal preference.
  7. Document a complete design process as a portfolio case study: problem → research → design → test → outcome.
  8. Apply AI-native UX workflows — generative prototyping, LLM-assisted research, and agentic design systems — and critically evaluate AI output against real user needs.
  9. Design for human-machine interaction scenarios — automation handoffs, shared-control interfaces, and multimodal feedback using haptic, spatial audio, and ambient light.
  10. Create invisible and Zero-UI experiences using eye-gaze, gesture, voice, and anticipatory design for screenless or ambient environments.
  11. Audit interfaces for algorithmic bias, cultural inclusivity, and ethical design — and propose interventions that keep AI-driven systems fair and transparent.
  12. Apply Privacy by Design and biophilic principles to create experiences that respect human attention cycles, well-being, and user consent.
  13. Engage critically with practitioners building future-facing products through Expert Sessions, and apply that insight directly to their own projects.

Standard Session Format

Each 3-hour core session follows this structure:

  • ~45 min: Concept input — tightly scoped, direct, no padding
  • ~90 min: Hands-on workshop: making, testing, or critiquing
  • ~45 min: Structured review — evidence check, peer feedback, next-session brief

Expert Session Format

Each 2-hour Expert Session follows this structure:

  • ~60 min: Practitioner talk — a real project, a real problem, a real decision they made
  • ~30 min: Live Q&A — unscripted, participant-driven
  • ~30 min: Application workshop — participants immediately apply one insight to their own project

Theory enters when the task demands it. Expert Sessions make the stakes real.

04

Curriculum Structure

Module 1: Understanding Users Sessions 1–3  ·  9 Hours
Session 1  ·  3 hrs

What UX Actually Is

  • Why interfaces fail: the gap between how builders think and how users behave
  • The five-component framework: research → IA → visual design → prototype → test
  • What UX work looks like across domains: fintech, health, e-commerce, civic, education
  • How to choose a project context: real problem, real users, real stakes
Workshop: Choose your project domain. Write a one-paragraph problem statement. Identify 3 types of users who would interact with this product or service.
Session 2  ·  3 hrs

User Research Methods

  • Structured interviews: question design, active listening, not leading the witness
  • Contextual inquiry: watching people do things, not asking what they would do
  • Research ethics: informed consent, privacy, working with vulnerable participants
  • Affinity mapping: turning 30 observations into 5 insights
Workshop: Write a 7-question interview guide for your project. Conduct 2 micro-interviews with willing participants from outside the room. Synthesize 3 insights on a physical affinity map.
Session 3  ·  3 hrs

Personas, Journey Maps, and Problem Definition

  • Building evidence-based personas: what the data says, not who you imagine
  • Journey mapping: every step a user takes, digital and physical, from first contact to goal completion
  • Pain point scoring: frequency × severity = where to focus
  • From insights to a sharp, testable problem statement (HMW framing)
Workshop: Build one primary persona and one journey map (minimum 6 steps) from your interview data. Score 3 pain points. Write a "How Might We" statement.
Module 1 Deliverable: Research synthesis document — 3+ user insights, 1 persona, 1 journey map, 1 HMW problem statement.
Expert Session 1  ·  2 hrs

AI in UX — What's Real, What's Hype

Format: Practitioner talk → Q&A → application workshop

  • The AI tools practitioners actually use every day vs. tools they've tried and abandoned
  • The difference between AI that accelerates good work and AI that disguises lazy work
  • How to brief an AI tool the same way you would brief a junior designer
  • What senior designers and hiring managers look for in someone who works fluently with AI
  • Where AI is genuinely changing the designer's role — and where it is not
Application Workshop (30 min): Participants review their own research from Module 1. Identify one part of their research or synthesis workflow where AI could have helped — or where they tried it and it fell short. Write a specific, one-paragraph observation.

Expert profile: Senior product designer, AI tools researcher, or design lead actively integrating AI into a production design workflow. I will source and brief all speakers; confirmed with REVA.

Module 2: Structure and Visual Design Sessions 4–6  ·  9 Hours
Session 4  ·  3 hrs

Information Architecture and Task Flows

  • What IA is: the skeleton of any digital product
  • Site maps and content hierarchies: what lives where and why
  • Task flows: mapping the path from user intent to completed goal — including decision points, errors, and dead ends
  • Card sorting: letting users organise content, not guessing how they will
Workshop: Draft a site map or task flow for your product's primary use case. Run a 5-person paper card sort. Note where participants disagreed with your original structure.
Session 5  ·  3 hrs

Visual Design and UI Principles

  • Norman's affordances: objects that communicate how they work
  • Gestalt principles in layouts: proximity, hierarchy, alignment, repetition
  • Typography in UI: scale, weight, and line length for readability
  • Colour in UI: contrast ratios (WCAG AA), semantic colour, accessible palettes
  • Design systems: what they are, why they exist, how to use one
Workshop: Evaluate any app or website you use regularly against Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics. Find and document 5 violations with severity ratings (1–4 scale).
Session 6  ·  3 hrs

Interaction Patterns and Design for India

  • Common UI patterns: navigation, forms, onboarding, empty states, error messages
  • Gesture, voice, and haptic interaction: when each mode earns its place
  • Designing for India's reality: variable connectivity, low-end devices, multilingual users, mixed literacy
  • Inclusive design: not a constraint, the default condition
Workshop: Apply 3 UI patterns to your product. Produce annotated mid-fidelity wireframes (paper or digital) for your 2 most critical screens.
Module 2 Deliverable: Annotated wireframes for the primary task flow + heuristic audit with severity scores.
Expert Session 2  ·  2 hrs

HMI and Emerging Interaction Paradigms

Format: Practitioner talk → Q&A → application workshop

  • Designing human-machine handoffs at scale — autonomous vehicles, clinical AI, industrial systems
  • Multimodal interfaces beyond the screen: haptic, voice, gaze, and ambient light as UX layers
  • Anticipatory and Zero-UI design: systems that act before the user commands
  • What it looks like to ship a product in one of these domains — tools, constraints, team structure
  • What skills are already in demand in HMI that most UX programs aren't teaching
Application Workshop (30 min): Participants review their wireframes from Module 2. Identify one interaction in their design that could be made more anticipatory, multimodal, or contextually aware. Sketch the revised interaction and write a one-paragraph rationale.

Expert profile: Product designer or UX lead from automotive, medtech, voice UI, or spatial computing with direct experience shipping non-screen or multi-modal products. May be drawn from my professional network or sourced through REVA's industry connections.

Module 3: Prototyping and Testing Sessions 7–10  ·  12 Hours
Session 7  ·  3 hrs

Figma and Prototyping Fundamentals

  • Figma environment: frames, layers, components, variants, auto-layout
  • Building a design system from scratch: colour tokens, type styles, component library
  • Interactive prototyping: connecting screens, triggers, transitions
  • Fidelity decisions: when paper is the right tool, when Figma is, when code is
Workshop: Build a complete clickable Figma prototype for your primary task flow — minimum 5 connected screens, using a component library you create during the session.
Session 8  ·  3 hrs

Usability Testing Methods

  • Moderated usability testing: facilitator role, task writing, think-aloud protocol
  • Unmoderated testing: when to use it and what you miss
  • Metrics that matter: task success rate, time-on-task, error rate, System Usability Scale (SUS)
  • Recruiting participants: how to find 5 people who are not your friends
Workshop: Write a 5-task usability test script for your prototype. Conduct one moderated test session with a participant from outside your team. Document findings on a structured observation sheet.
Session 9  ·  3 hrs

AI in UX: Tools, Workflows, and Critical Thinking

  • How AI is reshaping the UX workflow: generation, evaluation, and automation across research, design, and testing
  • Generative prototyping: using AI models (Figma AI, Uizard, Galileo, Relume) to produce UI variants — and evaluating output quality critically
  • Algorithmic user research: LLMs synthesising interview notes, generating affinity clusters, identifying behavioral patterns — and where they hallucinate or flatten nuance
  • Agentic design systems: AI agents that automate token consistency, component suggestion, and style guide maintenance
  • Dynamic personalization: designing for ML-driven interfaces that adapt layout, content, and navigation in real-time
  • AI in usability testing: automated heuristic analysis, sentiment analysis on feedback — what the tools miss that a human moderator catches
  • Ethics of AI in UX: bias in generated interfaces, over-reliance on AI personas vs. real user research, transparency with clients about AI use
Workshop: Take one screen from your Figma prototype. Generate an AI alternative using a tool of your choice. Side-by-side critique: what did the AI get right, what did it get wrong, and why? Document your findings as a 1-page design decision note.
Session 10  ·  3 hrs

Iteration Lab

  • Reading test data without over-reading it: separating signal from noise
  • Structured critique: before/after comparison from evidence, not from opinion
  • Version management: annotated change logs as part of your process document
  • What "done" looks like: criteria for a complete, testable design
Workshop: Apply a minimum of 3 changes to your prototype driven directly by usability test findings. Document each change: what the finding was, what you changed, why.
Module 3 Deliverable: Tested and iterated Figma prototype + usability test report (n ≥ 5, success rate and SUS score documented) + AI design decision note.
Expert Session 3  ·  2 hrs

Career in Future-Forward UX — Panel

Format: Panel (2–3 practitioners) → open Q&A → portfolio review session. I will moderate.

  • What skills are already in demand in AI-native design, voice UI, spatial computing, and autonomous systems that degree programs aren't teaching
  • How to build a portfolio that signals future-readiness rather than execution of past trends
  • The first hire question: what a junior UX role in an emerging area actually requires
  • What the design job market in India looks like right now — roles, salaries, emerging paths
  • Live portfolio feedback: participants share their work; panel responds to what they see
Application Workshop (30 min): Each participant shares their draft case study structure (1 page, no visuals required). Peers and practitioners give structured feedback on clarity and evidence strength. Participants leave with 3 specific revisions to make before final submission.

Expert profile: 2–3 practitioners from voice UI, spatial computing, AI-native products, or emerging interaction domains. At least one should have been involved in hiring junior designers for a future-facing UX role in the past 2 years. I will moderate the panel.

Module 4: Future-Ready UX and Portfolio Sessions 11–12  ·  6 Hours
Session 11  ·  3 hrs

Emerging Frontiers + Case Study Workshop

Part 1 — Four Emerging Areas in UX (90 min)

  • Advanced Human-Machine Interaction: trust and transparency in automation, designing system confidence displays and handoff moments, multimodal feedback using haptic, spatial audio, and peripheral lighting
  • Invisible UX and Zero-UI: somatic inputs (eye-gaze, gesture, voice), anticipatory design for systems that act before the user commands, ambient intelligence and information architecture without screens
  • Ethical and Biophilic UX: auditing AI-driven interfaces for algorithmic bias and cultural inclusivity, Privacy by Design in biometric-rich environments, circadian-aligned interaction rhythms and digital well-being
  • The designer's integrated responsibility: how these areas intersect and what they collectively demand from a future-ready practitioner

Part 2 — Case Study Construction (90 min)

  • The portfolio case study as an evidence document, not a mood board
  • Structure: problem → research → decisions → prototype → test results → outcomes
  • Writing interaction rationale: "I did this because the data showed..." not "I did this because it looks better"
  • Formatting for different audiences: portfolio website vs. job interview vs. client presentation
  • What to include when you only have student projects: honesty about constraints is a strength
Workshop: Write your complete case study in long form. Every design decision must have a one-sentence rationale grounded in research or test data. Peer review in pairs: does the evidence support each claim?
Session 12  ·  3 hrs

Final Jury

  • Formal presentation of completed prototype and process documentation
  • Jury panel: myself + REVA faculty member + at least one invited industry practitioner
  • Format: 8 minutes presentation + 7 minutes Q&A per participant or team
  • Certificates issued to participants meeting passing thresholds on all components
Module 4 Deliverable: Final working prototype + complete portfolio case study + jury presentation.

It does not need to be a built product — many of the strongest projects are design explorations of systems that exist poorly today, or future scenarios the participant has a well-grounded reason to believe are coming.

Participants come in mid-project; they hear how practitioners navigated similar problems; they immediately apply one takeaway to their own work. The 30-minute application workshop at the end of every Expert Session is mandatory and assessed.

Minimum Evidence Standards

The following thresholds must be met for a passing outcome:

Work that does not meet these thresholds is incomplete regardless of visual quality or effort.

Grading Dimensions

Each deliverable is evaluated on three equal dimensions:

  1. Evidence quality: Is the design decision supported by observation or data, not assumption or preference?
  2. Design reasoning: Does the decision follow logically from the evidence, and is the logic explained?
  3. Execution: Is the prototype functional and testable by a stranger with no prior briefing?

08

Teaching Methodology

Task-First, Theory-Second

The program does not front-load theory and then apply it. Theory enters at the moment the task demands it. Week 1 begins with a real problem and real users. Concepts are introduced because participants need them to proceed, not because they appear next in a syllabus.

30 / 70 Ratio

30% structured input. 70% workshop, testing, and critique. This ratio is protected. Lecture time is not expanded to fill workshop time.

Evidence Culture

Every design decision requires a one-sentence rationale grounded in research or test data. "It looks better" is not a rationale. "Two out of five test participants missed the button because it lacked sufficient contrast" is a rationale.

Team Structure

  • Research phases (Module 1): individual or pairs
  • Design and prototype (Modules 2–3): teams of 2–3, mixed backgrounds where possible
  • Final portfolio and jury: individual
