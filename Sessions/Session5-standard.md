# THE STANDARD: MAKE THIS AN EXCEPTIONAL UX DESIGN WORKSHOP

This should not look or feel like a conventional classroom presentation, a generic Figma tutorial, or an AI-generated collection of slides.

Build an immersive, visually rich, practical UX/UI design masterclass that feels like a premium product design studio running a live design challenge.

The experience should make students curious, encourage participation, teach meaningful design thinking and help them understand how a real digital product is conceived, designed, tested and improved.

The quality bar is exceptionally high.

## 1. VISUAL STORYTELLING — NOT JUST SLIDE DESIGN

Every slide, activity and learning section must have a clear visual purpose.

Avoid:
- Slides filled with paragraphs.
- Repetitive layouts.
- Generic stock illustrations.
- Decorative gradients without purpose.
- Generic AI-generated business graphics.
- Unnecessary icons and visual clutter.
- Screenshots used without explanation.
- Text-heavy explanations of concepts that could be demonstrated visually.

Instead, use:
- Large editorial headlines.
- Real product interfaces.
- Annotated screenshots.
- Before-and-after comparisons.
- Journey maps.
- User flow diagrams.
- Component anatomy.
- Interaction examples.
- Visual explanations of design decisions.
- Contextual scenarios.
- Interactive challenges.
- Realistic product situations.

Use a combination of dark editorial presentation layouts and carefully composed visual demonstrations.

Maintain a consistent 16:9 presentation format.

Every visual should help students understand something.

## 2. CONTEXT IS MORE IMPORTANT THAN DECORATION

Never teach a design concept in isolation when it can be demonstrated through Mindspace.

For example:

Instead of explaining a text field with a definition, show:
- Arya entering her name during onboarding.
- The field in its default state.
- The focused state.
- An invalid input state.
- A helpful validation message.
- The correct component choice and reasoning.

Instead of explaining bottom navigation theoretically, show how Arya moves between Home, Discover, Journal and Profile.

Instead of explaining cognitive load through paragraphs, demonstrate a cluttered meditation home screen and a simplified alternative.

Every major UX/UI concept should have:

1. A real user situation.
2. The design problem.
3. The relevant design principle.
4. A visual demonstration.
5. A practical student challenge.
6. A takeaway.

Teach students WHY a design decision works, not simply HOW to reproduce it.

## 3. USE AUTHENTIC DESIGN REFERENCES

Inspect the connected Figma resources before creating teaching material.

Use:
- Actual Mindspace screens.
- Mindspace styles and components.
- The connected Material Design 3 Figma kit.
- Actual component variants and states.
- Existing typography, colour and spacing references.

Material Design 3 is the learning reference.

Mindspace is the product being designed.

The rule is:

LEARN WITH MATERIAL. DESIGN WITH MINDSPACE.

Do not fabricate screenshots or claim that an asset comes from a Figma library unless it has actually been inspected.

If an asset is inaccessible, clearly identify the limitation and create an appropriate alternative explanation.

## 4. ONE USER. ONE PRODUCT. ONE DESIGN LANGUAGE.

The shared persona across all eight teams is:

ARYA
Age: 30
Occupation: Working professional
Experience: New to meditation.

Arya wants to manage stress, sleep better, improve focus and develop a sustainable meditation habit.

She has a busy schedule and usually has 5–15 minutes available.

She needs a simple, reassuring experience without complicated terminology or unnecessary choices.

All eight teams must design different stages of Arya's journey.

Do not introduce additional competing primary personas.

All teams must follow the same Mindspace design system:
- Colours.
- Typography.
- Spacing.
- Components.
- Icons.
- Interaction patterns.
- Navigation.
- Tone of voice.

The final output must feel like one application designed by one product team.

## 5. MAKE THE WORKSHOP PRACTICAL AND PARTICIPATORY

Students should never spend long periods passively watching slides.

Build participation into the presentation.

Include:
- Questions to ask the room.
- Short individual exercises.
- Team discussions.
- Component identification games.
- Design decision challenges.
- Broken UX scenarios.
- Time-bound challenges.
- Peer feedback.
- Facilitator discussion prompts.
- Answer reveals with explanations.

Each activity must contain:
- Objective.
- Instructions.
- Duration.
- Expected student output.
- Facilitator notes.
- Explanation or learning takeaway.

All interactive controls must actually work.

## 6. DESIGN THE PRESENTATION AS A STORY

The presentation should have a deliberate narrative.

ACT 1 — THE MINDSET
Why UX matters. Understand the user before designing the interface.

ACT 2 — THE TOOL
Explore Figma through hands-on learning.

ACT 3 — THE LANGUAGE
Understand components, design systems and visual consistency.

ACT 4 — THE CHALLENGE
Introduce Arya, Mindspace and the eight team missions.

ACT 5 — THE CREATION
Research, define, map, sketch and design.

ACT 6 — THE PRODUCT
Connect eight journeys into one application.

ACT 7 — THE CRITIQUE
Evaluate the experience through usability and design principles.

ACT 8 — THE EVOLUTION
Improve the product based on feedback.

ACT 9 — THE SHOWCASE
Present the final experience and celebrate thoughtful design decisions.

The narrative should build anticipation and make every stage feel connected.

## 7. TEACH THE DESIGNER'S MENTAL TOOLKIT THROUGHOUT

Do not isolate UX theory into one long lecture.

Introduce design principles when they become relevant.

Include:
- User-centred design.
- Information architecture.
- User journeys.
- Cognitive load.
- Visual hierarchy.
- Affordances and signifiers.
- Feedback and system status.
- Consistency.
- Accessibility.
- Error prevention and recovery.
- Recognition over recall.
- Progressive disclosure.
- User control.
- Empty, loading, error and success states.

Use Mindspace examples to demonstrate each principle.

## 8. MAKE THE TEAM CHALLENGE FEEL LIKE A REAL PRODUCT ASSIGNMENT

Each team must receive:
- Its mission.
- Arya's relevant situation.
- Preliminary research insights.
- The problem to solve.
- A How Might We question.
- Expected journey.
- Constraints.
- Required deliverables.
- Success criteria.

Label synthesized research insights as workshop hypotheses, not verified interview findings.

Teams must explain their problem before opening Figma.

They must design connected flows rather than disconnected screens.

## 9. PRODUCT ASSEMBLY IS NON-NEGOTIABLE

At the end of the first design sprint, bring all eight journeys together.

Show how they connect.

Identify:
- Shared navigation.
- Common components.
- Journey handoffs.
- Entry and exit points.
- Inconsistent terminology.
- Duplicate patterns.
- Missing states.
- Broken connections.

The final Mindspace experience must be coherent.

Do not simply display eight separate team presentations and call it one product.

## 10. MAKE CRITIQUE AND REFINEMENT MEANINGFUL

Students should receive constructive feedback based on design reasoning.

Evaluate:
- Does it solve Arya's problem?
- Is the journey understandable?
- Is the primary action clear?
- Is the information necessary?
- Are components used appropriately?
- Is the interface consistent?
- Is it accessible?
- Does the experience handle errors and interruptions?
- What can be simplified?

Require every team to make meaningful improvements after critique.

Show before-and-after comparisons during the final showcase.

## 11. FACILITATOR EXPERIENCE

Build the application for actual live workshop use.

The facilitator must be able to:
- Navigate the presentation.
- See the session timeline.
- Start, pause and reset timers.
- View current activity instructions.
- Access speaker notes.
- Monitor team progress.
- Track scores.
- Run games.
- Reveal answers.
- Manage the final showcase.

The experience must work reliably during a six-hour in-person session.

Do not leave decorative buttons or incomplete interactions.

## 12. QUALITY ASSURANCE — BEFORE HANDOVER

Conduct a complete facilitator walkthrough.

Verify:

CONTENT
- All workshop sessions are present.
- All eight team briefs are complete.
- Arya is the only shared primary persona.
- All activities have instructions and outcomes.
- Timings add up to six hours.
- The learning narrative is coherent.

VISUALS
- Slides have meaningful visual variety.
- Authentic Mindspace assets are used.
- Material 3 references are accurate.
- Text is readable from a classroom screen.
- Visuals support the teaching content.
- No fabricated screenshots are presented as real assets.

FUNCTIONALITY
- Navigation works.
- Timers work.
- Activities work.
- Games work.
- Scoring works.
- Team progress works.
- Presentation mode works.
- State persists appropriately.

PRODUCT QUALITY
- Eight journeys connect.
- Design-system consistency is maintained.
- The product assembly is understandable.
- Critique and refinement are integrated.

Do not declare completion simply because the application renders.

Run the actual workshop experience from beginning to end.

Fix broken interactions, missing content, inconsistent styling and incomplete sections before handover.

## FINAL QUALITY BAR

Imagine this workshop being conducted at a leading design school.

Students should leave feeling that they have experienced how a real product design team works.

The presentation should be visually memorable.

The exercises should be engaging.

The design decisions should be meaningful.

The final product should feel coherent.

The facilitator should be able to run the entire six-hour workshop without having to invent missing content or improvise incomplete activities.

Do not optimise for the number of slides.

Optimise for the quality of learning.

BUILD THE COMPLETE EXPERIENCE.
