## 2. What HCI Means in an M.Arch Context

For a product team, HCI is about screens, flows, and feedback loops. For architects, the frame shifts but the discipline doesn’t.

Third-semester M.Arch students are already fluent in program, circulation, materiality, and representation. HCI adds a layer they will increasingly need in practice: **how digital interfaces, sensors, and services change the way people inhabit, navigate, and make decisions inside the spaces they design.**

I don’t see this as a “digital skills” add-on. I see it as extending spatial thinking into hybrid environments — lobbies with wayfinding apps, co-working spaces with booking systems, affordable housing with service-delivery interfaces, cultural buildings with layered digital exhibits. The technology is not decoration. It shapes behavior.

My working definition for this course, kept deliberately broad:

> HCI is the practice of designing human–technology relationships — with equal attention to research evidence, interaction quality, ethical consequence, and the physical context in which technology is experienced.

That maps to an integrated mix: digital product craft, research rigor, spatial consequence, and critical awareness of what technology does to people and places.

## 3. The Bridge I Propose: From Spatial Program to Interaction Program

Since architecture is the students’ native language, I would anchor the course on a bridge I’ve used implicitly throughout my career — **translating complexity into lived experience.**

At Cisco, I worked on the SRM platform: enterprise software with dense workflows, multiple user types, and high stakes if someone got lost in the interface. The design problem wasn’t visual styling. It was structural — who needs what, when, in what sequence, with what feedback. That is not far from how an architect reads a brief and distributes program across a plan.

At Rupeek, when we built the research lab, we weren’t just running usability tests on screens. We were watching how people in Indian households actually handled gold loans — where they hesitated, what they didn’t trust, what the physical and digital touchpoints were. Research and environment were inseparable.

At MoneyView today, the hardest design problems are often about trust, clarity under stress, and accessibility across literacy and device constraints. Again: not just UI, but behavior in context.

**For M.Arch students, I would frame the bridge this way:**

| Architecture studio habit | HCI parallel |
|---|---|
| Reading a site and its constraints | Contextual inquiry — understanding users in their real environment |
| Program distribution & circulation | Information architecture & task flows |
| Material & detail resolution | Interaction states, feedback, micro-interactions |
| Client/stakeholder presentation | Research synthesis & design rationale |
| Physical mock-up / model | Low-to-high fidelity prototype |
| Design review / jury | Usability testing & studio critique |

The course doesn’t ask architects to become app designers. It asks them to apply the design thinking they already have — observation, iteration, critique, representation — to problems where the “building” includes a digital layer.

**Suggested studio themes** (to be aligned with program coordinator):

1. **Wayfinding & navigation** — a campus, hospital, or transit hub where physical signage and digital guidance must work together  
2. **Smart environment interface** — a workplace, home, or public building typology the cohort is already studying, with a control/booking/information layer  
3. **Community-facing service** — housing, civic, or cultural context where the interface mediates access (informed by participatory design methods)

These keep the work spatially grounded rather than abstract “app design exercises.”

## 4. How I Teach

### Format
Workshop- and task-based. Students learn by doing, with short inputs before each sprint. Roughly **30% structured theory / 70% making and testing** — enough conceptual grounding to avoid guesswork, but never a course that feels like a seminar about design.

### Theory — how much, and which canon
I don’t believe in front-loading Nielsen heuristics and Norman’s affordances as a syllabus checklist. Students forget that. I’d introduce theory **when a project needs it**:

- **Affordances & mental models** — when students are prototyping a control interface for an environment  
- **Heuristic evaluation** — when they critique each other’s work in week 4–5  
- **Inclusive design principles** — when accessibility becomes a real constraint, not a slide  
- **Research ethics** — before any field study or user conversation  

Classic references (Norman, Rogers & Sharp, selected papers) would sit alongside architecture-adjacent readings on experience, behavior, and technology in built space. Theory serves the task. Not the other way around.

### Studio culture
Critiques would follow architecture school norms — pin-up or screen presentation, peer feedback, structured faculty response — not a corporate design review. I’ve sat through enough of both to know the difference. Architecture students are already good at defending spatial decisions. I’d push them to defend **interaction decisions** with the same rigor: what did you observe, what did you test, what changed.

### Collaboration
Pairs for early exercises (research pairs, critique partners). Teams of 3–4 for the main project — large enough to divide research, IA, visual, and prototyping work; small enough that everyone stays accountable.

### Tools
Comfortable teaching with: **Figma/FigJam, Adobe XD/Illustrator, Miro, HTML/CSS/JS for quick interactive prototypes**, and usability testing tools (Maze, Optimal Workshop, or equivalent). Tool choice would follow project needs, not the reverse. Paper prototypes stay on the table — they’re often faster and more honest in week one.

### Guest exposure
I’d bring in practitioners across UX research, spatial/tech practice, and development — not as a lecture series, but timed to project milestones (e.g., a researcher before fieldwork week, a developer before prototype handoff). Priority order: **industry UX practitioners → architects or designers working on spatial/digital hybrids → technologists who can speak to feasibility.**

## 5. What Students Will Be Able to Do

By the end of the engagement, students should be able to:

| Outcome | Emphasis |
|---|---|
| Plan and conduct basic user research (interviews, observation, synthesis) | High |
| Map tasks, flows, and information architecture for a real context | High |
| Build and iterate prototypes — low-fi to interactive | High |
| Critique interfaces and hybrid experiences using HCI frameworks | Medium |
| Propose technology-integrated concepts grounded in spatial/program logic | High |
| Present and defend decisions with evidence, not taste | High |

The capstone deliverable I care most about: **a working prototype** — not a polished Keynote concept. Something a user can actually click through, walk through, or interact with, backed by documented research and at least one round of testing.

Example of what “working prototype” means at different fidelity:
- A clickable Figma flow for a building visitor app, tested with 5 users  
- A simple HTML prototype for a kiosk or booking interface  
- A physical-digital mock-up (e.g., Arduino-triggered feedback for an installation concept) if the studio brief calls for it

## 6. Illustrative Examples from My Practice

I’d bring these into the classroom not as war stories, but as case references students can map to their own projects.

**Cisco SRM — complexity made navigable**  
Enterprise software with multiple modules and user roles. The lesson for architects: when a system has many moving parts, you need a clear hierarchy and predictable paths. Same logic as a mixed-use building where circulation can’t be an afterthought.

**Rupeek research lab — evidence before pixels**  
We didn’t start with UI. We started with watching real people. The lab gave the team a repeatable way to test assumptions. I’d run a condensed version of this with students: one week of observation and synthesis before anyone opens Figma.

**DTALK — 30+ brand projects, tight timelines**  
Consulting taught me how to scope, prototype fast, and present options clearly. Useful when students have six weeks and a jury at the end.

**MoneyView — trust, clarity, scale**  
Fintech design in India means designing for anxiety, low patience, and uneven digital literacy. Accessibility and inclusive design aren’t elective topics here — they’re survival requirements. Directly relevant when students design interfaces for public or community-facing spaces.

## 7. What I Bring That’s Distinctive

Most HCI courses in design schools are taught by academics or pure UX practitioners. My position is different:

- **I’ve built design teams and research infrastructure**, not just delivered projects. I know what “good process” looks like when there’s no textbook answer.  
- **I’ve worked inside large product organizations (SAP, Cisco) and high-growth startups (Rupeek, MoneyView).** Students get industry realism — how decisions actually get made, what gets cut, what matters in a review.  
- **I have an engineering degree.** I can speak to technical feasibility without pretending to be a developer, and I can push students past purely visual solutions.  
- **I’m comfortable in the workshop.** This won’t be a slide-deck semester.

Integrated value for REVA’s M.Arch program: employability (digital literacy for practice), design excellence (portfolio differentiation), research orientation (evidence-based proposals), and social relevance (inclusive, context-aware design for Indian users and environments).

## 9. Closing Note

Architecture education already trains students to think about human experience in physical space. HCI gives them a structured vocabulary and method set for the digital and hybrid layer that practice is already demanding — smart buildings, civic tech, experiential culture, workplace platforms, housing services.

I’m not proposing to turn M.Arch students into UX designers. I’m proposing to make them **better architects for the environments they’ll actually build** — where screens, sensors, and services are part of the design problem, not someone else’s problem.

I’d welcome a conversation with the program coordinator to align the studio brief with the semester’s studio theme and finalize the weekly structure.
