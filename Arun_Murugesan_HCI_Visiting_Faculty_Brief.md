**Arun Murugesan** · Head of Design, MoneyView

**Cohort:** 15–16 students · 16 weeks · 2 contact hours/week

## The question I keep coming back to

Architecture schools train students to defend every millimetre of a section drawing. They can explain why a corridor widens, why light enters at a particular angle, why circulation bends the way it does. Evidence is spatial. It is visible on the sheet.

Then we send them into practice, where the buildings they design increasingly ship with booking systems, visitor apps, access control interfaces, AR overlays, environmental dashboards, and collaboration tools, and we expect them to treat those as someone else's problem.

I don't think that's sustainable. And I don't think it requires turning architects into product designers.

It requires a different kind of bridge.

---

## What the data actually says

This isn't intuition. The shift is already measurable.

- **Smart building technology** is projected to exceed $100B globally by 2026, with occupancy analytics, environmental controls, and tenant-facing apps becoming standard in commercial and institutional projects: not luxury add-ons.
- **Usability research** consistently shows that 88% of users are less likely to return after a poor digital experience (Amazon Web Services / industry benchmarks). A beautifully resolved lobby with a confusing check-in kiosk fails at the same threshold as a bad app.
- **Accessibility:** roughly 15% of the world's population lives with some form of disability (WHO). In India, designing for varying literacy, device constraints, and multilingual contexts isn't inclusive design as a specialty; it's the default condition. I've spent the last four years at MoneyView and Rupeek designing for exactly this reality.
- **REVA's own program outcomes** already anticipate this: PO4 explicitly calls for *research methodologies and data-driven approaches to inform design decisions*. PO2 asks students to *analyze user needs, contextual data, and environmental parameters*. The syllabus isn't asking for decoration. It's asking for evidence.

The gap isn't in the curriculum document. It's in how we connect architectural thinking to interaction thinking, with the same rigour students already bring to studio.

## My proposition: treat the interface as part of the spatial program

After 16–20 years across SAP, Cisco, DTALK, Rupeek, and MoneyView, plus an MS in Computer Software Engineering from BITS Pilani, and I've learned one thing that transfers everywhere:

**Complexity doesn't become simple through styling. It becomes simple through structure.**

At Cisco, I worked on the SRM platform: enterprise software where users got lost not because the UI was ugly, but because the program was wrong. Who sees what, when, with what feedback. At Rupeek, I built a user research lab and watched how Indian households actually navigated gold loans: the physical branch, the phone call, the app screen, all one journey. At MoneyView today, trust and clarity under stress are design problems measured in drop-off rates, task completion, and support tickets, not opinions.

Architects already do this with space. HCI asks the same questions when the "room" is partly digital.

| What architects already do | What UI/UX adds |
|---|---|
| Site analysis | Contextual inquiry: users in their real environment |
| Program distribution | Information architecture: what lives where |
| Circulation diagram | Task flow: paths, decision points, dead ends |
| Section drawing | Journey cross-section: entry to exit, all touchpoints |
| Material palette | Design system: tokens, components, consistency |
| Mock-up / model | Prototype: testable, not just presentable |
| Design jury | Usability test: evidence, not applause |

The course doesn't replace the studio. It gives students a method to design the digital layer of the buildings they already imagine.

## Six project directions where architecture and UI/UX genuinely merge

These are not "app design exercises." Each one starts from a building, a site, or a spatial typology, and asks what digital layer the experience requires.

### 1. Wayfinding as spatial IA
**Building type:** Hospital, campus, transit hub  
**Task:** Map physical circulation, then design a digital navigation layer (kiosk, mobile, or both).  
**Data requirement:** Card-sort or tree-test with 8+ users. Measure findability success rate before and after iteration.  
**Why it works:** Architects already think in paths. This makes them defend those paths with user evidence.

### 2. Smart environment control
**Building type:** Co-working, residential, hospitality  
**Task:** Design an interface for lighting, booking, access, or climate, tied to a floor plan the student has already drawn.  
**Data requirement:** Task-completion testing (can a user accomplish X in under Y seconds?). Heuristic evaluation against WCAG 2.1 AA.  
**Reference from my practice:** At Rupeek, we learned that control interfaces fail when users don't understand what the system is doing. Same in a smart home panel.

### 3. Community service interface
**Building type:** Affordable housing, civic centre, healthcare access  
**Task:** Design a service-delivery flow (applications, appointments, payments) for the community the building serves.  
**Data requirement:** 5 stakeholder interviews + journey map. Identify pain points with frequency/severity scoring.  
**Reference:** MoneyView: designing for anxiety, low patience, and uneven digital literacy in Indian users.

### 4. Cultural / museum hybrid experience
**Building type:** Gallery, heritage site, exhibition pavilion  
**Task:** AR overlay, audio guide, or interactive exhibit layer for a space students are already studying.  
**Data requirement:** Prototype testing with think-aloud protocol. Document where users disengage.  
**Syllabus alignment:** CO2 (spatial interface design), CO5 (gesture, voice, haptic patterns).

### 5. Collaborative design tool for AEC
**Building type:** Any: the "building" is the workflow  
**Task:** Reimagine how architects, clients, and consultants review a project (think: Figma for spatial review, or a BIM-adjacent interaction layer).  
**Data requirement:** Comparative analysis of 3 existing tools. Usability benchmark on student's prototype.  
**Reference from my practice:** Cisco SRM: multiple user roles, dense workflows, clarity as survival.

### 6. Accessibility-first public interface
**Building type:** Government office, transit, public amenity  
**Task:** Design an inclusive interface for diverse abilities, languages, and literacy levels.  
**Data requirement:** Accessibility audit (automated + manual). Testing with at least 2 assistive-technology users or simulated constraints.  
**Syllabus alignment:** CO4, PO9 (ethics), PO10 (SDG-aligned inclusive access).

Students would select or be assigned one direction. The brief stays architectural. The deliverable includes a **working frontend prototype**.

## How I teach: task-based, data-backed, 70% making

I align with REVA's 16-week structure (1hr lecture + 1hr lab). But I don't teach HCI as a theory course with projects attached. I teach it as **a sequence of tasks where each week produces evidence**.

Theory enters when the task demands it, not before.

### The ratio
**30% structured input / 70% workshop and testing.** Short framing, then make. Introduce Norman's affordances when students are building controls. Introduce heuristics when they're critiquing each other. Introduce research ethics before anyone leaves the classroom with an interview script.

### The data contract
Every task must answer: *what did you observe, what did you measure, what changed because of it?*

Minimum evidence standards I'd hold the cohort to:

| Phase | Task | Data output |
|---|---|---|
| Weeks 1–2 | Context + users | 5 user interviews or 3 contextual observations, synthesized into insights |
| Weeks 3–4 | Structure | Site map + task flow, validated with card sort or tree test (n≥8) |
| Weeks 5–6 | Visual + interaction | Design system draft, heuristic self-audit |
| Week 7–8 | Mid-semester | Research report + design recommendations, presented with evidence |
| Weeks 9–11 | Prototype | Clickable or coded frontend: not static screens |
| Week 12–13 | Test | Usability test (n≥5), success rate + time-on-task documented |
| Weeks 14–15 | Iterate | Before/after comparison from test data |
| Week 16 | Final jury | Working prototype + portfolio case study |

This maps directly to REVA's assessment split: 30% continuous (weekly evidence), 25% mid-semester (research + recommendations), 35% final (tested prototype), 10% portfolio (process documentation).

### Studio culture, not corporate review
Critiques follow architecture school norms: pin-up, peer feedback, jury. I push students to defend interaction decisions the way they defend a façade detail: *here's what I tested, here's what failed, here's what I changed.*

Pairs for early research. Teams of 3–4 for the main build.

### Tools
Figma/FigJam, Miro, Adobe XD, HTML/CSS/JS for working prototypes, Maze or equivalent for testing. Paper first when it's faster. Code when click-through isn't enough.

## What students actually ship

I care about one thing above aesthetics: **a working frontend presentation**.

That means:

- A **clickable Figma prototype** at minimum: full flow, not hero screens  
- Ideally, a **simple HTML/CSS/JS build** for kiosk, mobile, or web interfaces: something that runs in a browser without explanation  
- For spatial/immersive directions: a **VR/AR prototype** (Unity/Unreal) where the syllabus expects it, but always tested, never demo-ware  
- A **research appendix**: interview notes, test results, iteration log  
- A **portfolio case study** structured as: problem → evidence → design → test → outcome

If it doesn't work when a stranger interacts with it, it's not finished.
